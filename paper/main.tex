\title{Transistor-Level Energy Analysis of Signal Processing Algorithms: Beyond Operation Counting}

\begin{document}
\ninept
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Accurate energy prediction for signal processing algorithms is critical for resource-constrained and energy-aware systems, yet existing approaches rely on floating-point operation counts that ignore memory hierarchy costs, sequential dispatch overhead, and hardware implementation details. We present a physics-grounded energy model based on Transistor Operations (TOs), validated across 37 signal processing algorithms spanning 8 categories on two NVIDIA GPUs (RTX 4090 and A100 SXM4). Our four-parameter model achieves $r^2 = 0.947$ (RTX 4090) and $r^2 = 0.982$ (A100) with 80\% head-to-head ranking accuracy. Nsight Compute hardware profiling validates key corrections including split-radix FFT operation counts (99.4\% match) and complex MAC reduction on FMA hardware (99.2\% match). We identify three distinct energy regimes for sequential algorithms, with a 3 to 7$\times$ per-step energy difference between Python-loop dispatch and fused-sequential execution. A systematic CPU vs.\ GPU comparison across all 37 algorithms reveals that sequential algorithms are 10 to 100$\times$ more energy-efficient on CPU, challenging the assumption that GPUs are universally superior for compute.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Energy efficiency, signal processing, GPU, transistor operations, power measurement
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =========================================================================

Energy consumption is a first-order design constraint for signal processing systems, from battery-powered edge devices to datacenter-scale inference pipelines. Practitioners routinely estimate computational cost by counting floating-point operations (FLOPs), yet this metric systematically misrepresents actual energy. It ignores three physical realities: (i)~memory accesses cost 5 to 200$\times$ more energy than arithmetic~\cite{Horowitz2014}, (ii)~sequential algorithms on GPUs incur per-step dispatch overhead that can dominate the computation itself, and (iii)~hardware implementations diverge from textbook operation counts. As a concrete example of~(iii), fused multiply-add units halve complex MAC costs, and cuFFT's split-radix algorithm uses 30\% fewer operations than the standard Cooley-Tukey formula.

Prior work on energy-aware computing has focused on neural network accelerators~\cite{Yang2017, Desoli2017} and coarse GPU-level power modeling~\cite{Bridges2016}. The Transistor Operations (TO) framework~\cite{AnonTOML2026} introduced physics-grounded energy units anchored to CMOS transistor switching costs at a 45\,nm reference node, achieving $r^2 = 0.961$ across seven deep learning architectures. A subsequent study extended the framework to containerized cloud workloads~\cite{AnonTOMLCloud2026}. Neither study addressed signal processing, where computational patterns differ substantially from neural network inference: FFT butterfly structures, recursive filters, iterative decompositions, and purely comparison-based workloads such as median filtering (which executes zero floating-point instructions on GPU hardware).

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep0pt
\item A four-parameter energy model for 37 signal processing algorithms across 8 categories, achieving $r^2 = 0.947$ (RTX 4090) and $r^2 = 0.982$ (A100 SXM4) with 80\% head-to-head ranking accuracy on both GPUs.
\item Hardware-validated corrections to standard operation counts, including split-radix FFT (99.4\% match to Nsight Compute instruction counts) and complex MAC on FMA hardware (99.2\% match), with corrections that propagate to all FFT-based algorithms.
\item Identification of three sequential energy regimes with a 3 to 7$\times$ per-step cost difference between Python-loop dispatch and fused-sequential execution: parallel, Python-loop dispatch, and fused-sequential.
\item A systematic CPU vs.\ GPU energy comparison across all 37 algorithms showing that sequential algorithms are 10 to 100$\times$ more efficient on CPU, with clear category-level crossover guidance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{TOML Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Transistor Operations}

A Transistor Operation (TO) is a unit of computational cost anchored to CMOS circuit-level energy measurements at a 45\,nm reference node~\cite{Horowitz2014}. At 45\,nm and 0.9\,V, Horowitz reports that a 32-bit floating-point multiply costs 3.7\,pJ and a 32-bit floating-point add costs 0.9\,pJ, giving a multiply-accumulate (MAC) cost of approximately 4.6\,pJ~\cite{Sze2017}. We define 1\,TO $\approx$ 1\,fJ, so that one FP32 MAC corresponds to 5{,}000\,TOs.

Table~\ref{tab:to_costs} lists the TO costs used in this work, identical to those in prior studies~\cite{AnonTOML2026, AnonTOMLCloud2026}. Each floating-point operation on a modern GPU dispatches a full fused multiply-add (FMA) unit regardless of whether the instruction is an add, multiply, or FMA~\cite{NsightCompute}, so all three map to a single MAC cost. Transcendental functions (exp, sin, sqrt) are evaluated via polynomial approximation or Newton-Raphson iteration, requiring 3 to 4 FMA cycles and hence proportionally higher TO costs. Memory costs are weighted separately: the table assigns 192\,TOs per 32-bit SRAM word and 10{,}000\,TOs per HBM word, reflecting the order-of-magnitude hierarchy measured by Horowitz (5\,pJ for 8\,KB SRAM, 640\,pJ for DRAM). These TO values are \emph{relative weights}, not direct energy predictions; the fitted coefficients $\alpha_c$ and $\alpha_m$ (Section~\ref{sec:framework_model}) absorb architecture-specific and process-node scaling.

\subsection{Four-Parameter Energy Model}
\label{sec:framework_model}

For each algorithm, we derive the compute TO count $T_c$ and memory TO count $T_m$ analytically from the algorithm's mathematical definition, making these counts architecture-independent. The total predicted energy for a GPU invocation is:
\begin{equation}
E = \alpha_c \cdot T_c + \alpha_m \cdot T_m + \alpha_o \cdot S_o + \alpha_f \cdot S_f
\label{eq:four_param}
\end{equation}
where $S_o$ is the number of CUDA kernel launches in Python-loop sequential algorithms, $S_f$ is the number of fused-sequential timesteps, and $\alpha_c, \alpha_m, \alpha_o, \alpha_f \geq 0$ are fitted via non-negative least squares (NNLS). The non-negativity constraint is physical: energy cannot decrease with additional computation, memory access, or sequential overhead.

Each term captures a distinct energy mechanism:

\textbf{Compute energy} ($\alpha_c \cdot T_c$). The operation counts $T_c$ follow from each algorithm's mathematical structure. For FFT, we use the split-radix formula $4N \log_2 N - 6N + 8$~\cite{Duhamel1990} rather than the Cooley-Tukey count, reducing the predicted operation count by 30\%. For complex-valued algorithms, we count 4\,FMAs per complex MAC instead of 8 real operations, matching FMA hardware behavior. Both corrections are validated against Nsight Compute~\cite{NsightCompute} instruction counts in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The coefficient $\alpha_c$ absorbs the ratio between mathematical operation counts and actual instruction throughput on each GPU.

\textbf{Memory energy} ($\alpha_m \cdot T_m$). We count HBM words read and written per invocation based on each algorithm's data access pattern (input, output, and intermediate buffers that spill to global memory). On-chip reuse within a single kernel (e.g., FFT butterfly in shared memory) is counted as SRAM. The fitted $\alpha_m$ is consistently larger than $\alpha_c$ across GPUs, reflecting the energy dominance of data movement.

\textbf{Python-loop dispatch} ($\alpha_o \cdot S_o$). Sequential algorithms (adaptive filters, state estimators) execute a Python loop that launches CUDA kernels on each iteration. We count $S_o = I \times K$ where $I$ is the number of outer loop iterations and $K$ is the number of kernel launches per iteration (ranging from 7 for LMS to 43 for UKF, determined by source code analysis). Counting kernel launches rather than loop iterations alone reduces the coefficient of variation across algorithms from 130\% to 34\%, because $K$ captures the per-iteration computational density.

\textbf{Fused-sequential} ($\alpha_f \cdot S_f$). Algorithms that process timesteps serially within a single C++/CUDA kernel (e.g., cuDNN LSTM at batch size $B=1$) avoid Python dispatch entirely but still incur per-timestep energy from serial execution at low GPU utilization. The ratio $\alpha_o / \alpha_f$ ranges from 2.8$\times$ to 6.9$\times$ across GPUs, confirming these are distinct mechanisms: $\alpha_o$ is dominated by host-side CPU dispatch cost, while $\alpha_f$ reflects GPU-side serial execution cost.

\subsection{Algorithm Selection}

We benchmark 37 signal processing algorithms across 8 categories (Table~\ref{tab:algorithms}), selected to span the computational diversity of the field: from embarrassingly parallel transforms to inherently sequential recursive filters and iterative estimation. All implementations use canonical libraries (PyTorch, SciPy, scikit-learn, PyWavelets, torchaudio)~\cite{PyTorch} rather than custom CUDA kernels, reflecting the implementations practitioners actually deploy.

Each algorithm is benchmarked at four signal lengths ($N = 256$, $1024$, $4096$, $16384$) in FP32 precision. GPU batch sizes are $B = 2048$ where parallelizable (transforms, filters, spectral, ML), with smaller $B$ for algorithms whose memory footprint scales with $B \times N$ (e.g., $B = 512$ for direct DFT, $B = 2$ for ESPRIT at $N = 1024$). CPU benchmarks use $B = 1$.

\subsection{Hardware and Power Measurement}

\textbf{GPUs.} We measure on two NVIDIA GPUs: an RTX 4090 and an A100 SXM4. GPU power is sampled via NVML at approximately 20\,Hz. The RTX 4090 yields 138 benchmark points and the A100 yields 126.

\textbf{CPU.} An Intel Core i9-14900HX (8 Performance + 16 Efficiency cores, 55\,W base TDP) is measured via RAPL ``CPU Package'' counters exposed through LibreHardwareMonitor at approximately 217\,Hz, yielding 147 benchmark points.

\textbf{Thermal settling.} Before each measurement, we wait for the processor temperature to stabilize within $\pm 1^\circ$C over a 5-second window. This prevents thermal transients from biasing power readings, as leakage current is exponential in temperature.

\textbf{Idle baseline.} Each algorithm measurement begins with a per-algorithm idle power measurement (3\,s for GPU, 10\,s for CPU), from which we compute $\Delta P = P_{\text{active}} - P_{\text{idle}}$. Per-algorithm baselines are necessary because idle power drifts with temperature across a multi-hour benchmark suite.

\textbf{Measurement loop.} Each algorithm executes continuously for at least 10\,s (GPU) or 2\,s (CPU), with 50 warmup iterations on GPU to trigger JIT compilation and cache fills. Energy per call is computed as $E = \Delta P \times t / n_{\text{calls}}$, where $t$ is the active measurement duration and $n_{\text{calls}}$ is the total number of algorithm invocations.

\subsection{Nsight Compute Validation}

To validate the TO operation counts against actual hardware instruction throughput, we profile 23 of the 37 algorithms using NVIDIA Nsight Compute~\cite{NsightCompute} at $B = 1$ on the RTX 4090. The profiler reports per-kernel FP32 and FMA instruction counts, which we aggregate across all kernels in a single algorithm invocation and compare against the TO model's predicted operation counts. This provides a ground-truth check independent of the energy model's fitted $\alpha$ coefficients.

\subsection{Model Accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:coefficients} reports the fitted coefficients for the four-parameter model (Eq.~\ref{eq:four_param}) on each GPU. The model achieves $r^2 = 0.947$ on the RTX 4090 (138 points) and $r^2 = 0.982$ on the A100 (126 points). Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} shows predicted versus measured energy on a log-log scale; points cluster tightly along the identity line across five orders of magnitude.

The cross-GPU coefficient ratios are physically interpretable. The compute coefficients $\alpha_c$ are similar (13.6 vs.\ 15.2\,fJ/TO), consistent with comparable FP32 throughput on both architectures. The memory coefficient $\alpha_m$ is 2.6$\times$ larger on the A100, consistent with its wider memory interface and higher uncounted memory overhead (ECC, memory controller scheduling). The dispatch overhead $\alpha_o$ is 3.1$\times$ larger on the RTX 4090 because its host CPU (i9-14900HX) has higher Python interpreter latency than the A100's server-class Xeon. The fused-sequential coefficient $\alpha_f$ is consistent across GPUs (55.7 vs.\ 45.3\,$\mu$J/step, ratio 1.2$\times$), as expected for a GPU-side cost.

\subsection{Head-to-Head Ranking}

To test whether the model correctly identifies the more energy-efficient algorithm in practical choices, we evaluate 30 head-to-head pairs (e.g., FIR direct vs.\ FIR FFT-based, CNN vs.\ LSTM denoiser, Kalman vs.\ UKF). The model correctly ranks 24 of 30 pairs (80\%) on both GPUs (Fig.~\ref{fig:headtohead}). Five of the six failures involve the Kalman vs.\ UKF comparison, where the model's single $\alpha_o$ coefficient cannot capture the 8$\times$ difference in per-launch compute density between these algorithms. The remaining failure is a measurement-noise tie at $N = 256$.

\subsection{Nsight Compute Validation}

The TO operation counts are validated against hardware instruction counts from Nsight Compute, independent of the fitted $\alpha$ coefficients. Two corrections to standard operation-counting formulas are confirmed:

\textbf{Split-radix FFT.} The standard Cooley-Tukey formula ($5N \log_2 N$ real FP ops) overpredicts cuFFT's actual FP32 instruction count by 30\%. The split-radix formula $4N \log_2 N - 6N + 8$~\cite{Duhamel1990} matches Nsight Compute at $N = 4096$: 172{,}040 predicted vs.\ 173{,}055 measured (99.4\%). This correction propagates to all nine FFT-based algorithms.

\textbf{Complex MAC on FMA hardware.} The textbook complex multiply-accumulate (8 real ops: 4 multiplies + 4 adds) overpredicts by 2$\times$ on FMA hardware, where each complex MAC requires exactly 4 FMA instructions. For direct DFT at $N = 1024$ ($N^2$ complex MACs): 4{,}194{,}304 predicted vs.\ 4{,}229{,}120 measured (99.2\%). The 0.8\% residual corresponds to thread-block reduction operations.

Figure~\ref{fig:ncu} plots predicted versus measured instruction counts for all 23 profiled algorithms. Algorithms with analytically tractable operation counts (FFT, DFT, convolution, matmul-based) fall on or near the identity line. Algorithms with implementation-dependent iteration counts (SVD, PCA) show 3 to 10$\times$ underprediction, because cuSOLVER's iterative QR decomposition has convergence-dependent cost that no fixed formula can capture~\cite{GolubVanLoan2013}. The TO model uses the Householder bidiagonalization lower bound ($6ND^2$) for these algorithms; the gap is absorbed by $\alpha_c$.

\subsection{Case Study: FIR Crossover}

A classic algorithm selection problem is whether to implement FIR filtering via direct convolution ($O(NM)$ MACs for filter length $M$) or FFT-based overlap-add ($O(N \log N)$). Figure~\ref{fig:fir} shows that both the TO model and measurements identify the crossover: direct convolution is cheaper at short filter lengths, while FFT convolution wins as $M$ grows. The TO model correctly predicts the crossover point because it accounts for cuFFT's split-radix cost and the memory overhead of the FFT approach.

\subsection{Three Sequential Energy Regimes}

Sequential algorithms on GPUs fall into three distinct regimes:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep0pt
\item \textbf{Parallel} ($S_o = 0, S_f = 0$): All computation executes in a single kernel launch. Energy is governed by $\alpha_c T_c + \alpha_m T_m$.
\item \textbf{Python-loop dispatch} ($S_o > 0$): Each timestep triggers a Python loop iteration launching $K$ CUDA kernels. Median per-launch cost: 385\,$\mu$J (RTX 4090), 125\,$\mu$J (A100).
\item \textbf{Fused-sequential} ($S_f > 0$): A single C++/CUDA kernel processes all $N$ timesteps serially. Per-step cost: 56\,$\mu$J (RTX 4090), 45\,$\mu$J (A100).
\end{enumerate}

The $\alpha_o / \alpha_f$ ratio ranges from 2.8$\times$ (A100) to 6.9$\times$ (RTX 4090), confirming that Python-loop dispatch is substantially more expensive per step than fused-sequential execution. This ratio is consistent with the physical picture: $\alpha_o$ includes host-side Python interpreter and CUDA driver overhead, while $\alpha_f$ reflects only GPU-side serial execution cost.

% Fig 7 placed before the section text
\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig7_cpu_vs_gpu}
\caption{CPU-to-GPU energy ratio by category. Ratios above 1 indicate the GPU is more efficient. Sequential algorithms (adaptive, estimation) are 10 to 100$\times$ more efficient on CPU.}
\label{fig:cpugpu}

We compare energy per signal between the i9-14900HX CPU ($B = 1$) and the RTX 4090 GPU (energy per call divided by batch size) across 129 common (algorithm, $N$) configurations. GPU energy is normalized by $B$ to obtain a fair per-signal comparison.

Figure~\ref{fig:cpugpu} shows the CPU-to-GPU energy ratio by algorithm category. Three regimes emerge:

\textbf{Parallel-batched algorithms} (transforms, filters, spectral, ML, compression): the GPU wins 84 of 85 comparisons, with a median efficiency advantage of 22$\times$. Batch parallelism amortizes kernel launch overhead across $B$ signals, and the GPU's throughput advantage dominates.

\textbf{Sequential algorithms} (adaptive filters, state estimation): the CPU wins all 32 comparisons. The GPU's Python-loop dispatch overhead ($\alpha_o = 385$\,$\mu$J/launch) makes sequential algorithms highly inefficient on GPU. NLMS is 100$\times$ more energy-efficient on CPU, where the per-step overhead is simply a function call rather than a cross-device kernel launch.

\textbf{Decomposition} (SVD, PCA, FastICA, NMF): mixed results, with the CPU winning 6 of 8 comparisons. These algorithms involve eigendecomposition or matrix factorization that does not parallelize across the batch dimension. SVD shows a crossover: the CPU wins at $N = 256$ (ratio 0.59$\times$) but the GPU wins at $N \geq 1024$ (ratio 2.45$\times$) as cuSOLVER amortizes its fixed overhead.

The GPU's advantage decreases with signal length: the median CPU-to-GPU ratio drops from 23$\times$ at $N = 256$ to 4.3$\times$ at $N = 16384$, because per-signal compute grows while batch amortization remains constant.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =========================================================================

% Fig 6 placed before the discussion text that references it

\textbf{Comparison to FLOP counting.} Standard FLOP-based energy models make three systematic errors that the TO framework corrects. First, they overcount FFT operations by 30\% (Cooley-Tukey vs.\ split-radix). Second, they overcount complex arithmetic by 2$\times$ on FMA hardware. Third, they entirely ignore sequential dispatch overhead, which dominates energy for adaptive and estimation algorithms. A single-parameter model $E = \alpha \cdot \text{TO}_{\text{total}}$ (equivalent to FLOP counting with calibration) achieves $r^2 < 0.6$ on our data; separating compute, memory, and dispatch terms is essential.

\textbf{Limitations.} The model has three known limitations. (1)~The single $\alpha_o$ coefficient cannot distinguish algorithms with different per-launch compute densities (e.g., Kalman at 15 launches/iter vs.\ UKF at 43), causing 5 of 6 ranking failures. A per-algorithm $\alpha_o$ would fix this but would sacrifice generalizability. (2)~Factorization algorithms (SVD, PCA) have implementation-dependent operation counts that no fixed formula can predict; the TO model uses analytical lower bounds and absorbs the gap through $\alpha_c$. (3)~CPU energy measurements at small $N$ have high relative noise ($\sim$37\% for lightweight algorithms at $N = 256$) due to idle power variability.

Figure~\ref{fig:crossgpu} compares per-algorithm energy between the two GPUs. Most algorithms show a consistent cross-GPU energy ratio within each category, supporting the claim that the TO framework transfers across architectures with only the $\alpha$ coefficients refitted.

\textbf{Practical implications.} The results suggest three guidelines for signal processing practitioners: (1)~Sequential algorithms should run on CPU, not GPU, unless a fused CUDA kernel is available (3 to 7$\times$ per-step energy reduction from kernel fusion). (2)~FFT-based algorithms should pad inputs to power-of-2 lengths; non-power-of-2 sizes incur up to 5$\times$ energy penalties from cuFFT's mixed-radix fallback. (3)~For algorithm selection decisions (e.g., FIR direct vs.\ FFT convolution), the TO model provides energy rankings that agree with measurement 80\% of the time without requiring hardware access.

Figure~\ref{fig:denoising} illustrates guideline~(3) for the denoising use case. The Wiener filter is 10 to 50$\times$ more energy-efficient than the CNN denoiser and 100 to 500$\times$ cheaper than the LSTM denoiser, while the Transformer denoiser is the most expensive. The TO model correctly ranks all four approaches, providing practitioners with energy-aware algorithm selection without dedicated measurement infrastructure.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =========================================================================

We have presented a four-parameter energy model grounded in transistor-level operation costs that predicts GPU energy consumption for 37 signal processing algorithms with $r^2 \geq 0.947$ across two GPU architectures. Nsight Compute profiling validates that standard operation-counting assumptions (Cooley-Tukey FFT, 8-op complex MAC) systematically overpredict by 30 to 100\%, and that corrected formulas match hardware instruction counts to within 1\%. The identification of three sequential energy regimes, with per-step costs differing by 3 to 7$\times$ between Python-loop dispatch and fused-sequential execution, provides a principled basis for deciding when to use GPU vs.\ CPU execution. Our systematic comparison across 37 algorithms confirms that sequential signal processing algorithms are 10 to 100$\times$ more energy-efficient on CPU, a result with direct implications for energy-aware system design.
